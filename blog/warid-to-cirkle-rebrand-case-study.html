Case Study Series - 2: Warid to Cirkle: Identity Crisis or Strategic Evolution?

By Fardin Rahman Khan Raafi · November 27, 2026 · 5 min read · Brand Strategy
TL;DR Warid, Airtel, Robi and now Cirkle: a Bangladesh telecom rebrand case study on borrowed brand equity, switching costs and youth positioning by Fardin Raafi.

Think about it. One SIM card, one 016 number, and four different names on the screen in under twenty years. Warid. Airtel. Airtel by Robi. And now, Cirkle.

Most brands spend decades trying to build one name people trust. This one kept changing its name and kept its customers anyway.

A name can change four times. The number in your pocket never did, and that is the whole story.

So is this a brand that never knew who it was? Or one of the smartest long games in Bangladeshi telecom? Let's break it down.

1. Warid: the fastest start in the market (2007)

Warid Telecom, owned by the UAE based Abu Dhabi Group, paid US$50 million for Bangladesh's sixth mobile licence in December 2005 and went live on 10 May 2007.

The launch was aggressive. Warid hit 1 million subscribers in 70 days and was the fourth largest operator in two years. Big network, fast growth, but a late entrant fighting giants. That gap is what made the next move happen.

2. Airtel: borrowing a global name (2010)

In 2010, Bharti Airtel bought 70% of Warid for $300 million and the brand became Airtel.

This was a classic borrowed equity play. Instead of building trust from zero, you plug into a name that already means something across South Asia and Africa. Airtel then did the local work itself, positioning hard on youth, cheap data and "friends" culture. Over 16 years, it became one of the few telecom brands young people actually felt something for.

3. Airtel by Robi: the awkward decade (2016 to 2026)

In 2016, Airtel merged with Robi. Legally it became one company. But Robi did something smart: it kept Airtel alive as its youth sub-brand, on the same network.

Two brands, one infrastructure. Robi for the mass market, Airtel for the young. Smart segmentation, but it came with a catch.

Grameenphone and Banglalink complained to the regulator that Robi's continued use of the Airtel name broke the 2016 merger conditions, one of which required marketing under the Robi name. Robi said the matter was fully resolved and approved. Still, when you are renting a name you don't own, someone can always question the lease.

4. Cirkle: owning the house instead of renting it (2026)

On 17 August 2026, Robi officially retired Airtel and launched Cirkle. Same 016 numbers, same SIMs, same balances, same packages. Only the identity changed.

Robi frames it as the next step for a young, digital first generation, with more focus on affordable digital services and AI.

Here is the part most people miss. The name "Airtel" always belonged to someone else. Cirkle belongs to Robi. Every taka spent building it now builds Robi's own asset, not a partner's.

It also echoes Airtel's own old tagline, the network of friends. A circle of friends. The memory carries over even when the logo doesn't.

The marketer's take

If you work in branding, this case gives you three practical lessons:

  • Protect the switching cost, not the logo. Cirkle kept every 016 number. Customers lost nothing, so there was no reason to leave. If your rebrand asks users to do work, you will lose some of them.
  • Borrowed equity has an expiry date. Licensing a big name helps you start fast. But at some point you are building someone else's brand with your budget. Plan the exit early.
  • A new name doesn't fix an old experience. According to BTRC data, Robi received the most complaints of the four operators between January 2021 and June 2026. A fresh logo gets attention. Only better service keeps it.

Globally, we've seen this before. Dunkin' dropped "Donuts" from its name to signal it was more than one product. It worked because the coffee was already there. The rebrand followed the reality, not the other way around. (I broke down another culture-first brand move in why brands pay to get roasted.)

So, identity crisis or strategic evolution?

Honestly? Both, depending on when you looked.

From 2016 to 2026, it did look like an identity crisis. Two brands on one network, a name owned by a shareholder, and competitors questioning whether it was even allowed.

But Cirkle is the move that resolves it. It turns a borrowed name into an owned one while keeping the one thing customers actually cared about, their number.

The strategy is sound. The real test is not the logo launch at Sonargaon. It is whether a 20 year old in Rajshahi gets better data speed next month.
